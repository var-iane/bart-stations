Some of BART's at-risk stations serve riders who need it most

Ariane Vartanian

The Bay Area's embrace of remote work has hollowed out BART's finances. And now several of the stations at risk of closure serve riders who don't have the option to work from home.

Voters this November will decide on a sales tax measure that would fund the region's transit agencies and preserve their current operations. If they reject it, BART plans to shutter 15 stations sometime in 2027 to address an impending $376 million deficit.

Most of the targeted stations see fewer riders than the average BART station, making them straightforward choices from a financial perspective. But survey and census data suggest that some of the cuts would eliminate transit access for communities that are particularly likely to rely on it to get to work.

Of the 15 stations slated to close, five draw a greater share of low-income daily commuters—defined by BART as riders with household incomes under $50,000 who commute five or more days a week—than the systemwide average, according to the agency's 2024 station profile survey. Three of those five are clustered along the northeastern edge of Contra Costa County, at the far end of the Yellow Line: Pittsburg/Bay Point, Pittsburg Center, and Antioch.

Pandemic ridership data reveals who uses BART because they have no alternative. In April 2020, the first full month of shelter-in-place orders, weekday BART ridership collapsed to around 6% of its 2019 average. But the drop wasn't equal across the system, or even across Contra Costa County.

At Orinda, Lafayette, and Walnut Creek—stations serving wealthier cities closer to the Oakland and San Francisco metro areas—ridership plummeted that April to 2–4% of their 2019 weekday averages. Meanwhile, ridership at Antioch, Pittsburg Center, and Pittsburg/Bay Point held several times higher, at roughly 11–15%. This gap persisted through the rest of the pandemic.

Census survey data helps explain why. In the Orinda–Lafayette–Walnut Creek belt, approximately 70–80% of employed residents work in “management, business, science, and arts” occupations—fields including software engineering, law, and media that often lend themselves to remote work—compared to only about 15–30% in the northeastern Contra Costa communities. There, more residents work in service, construction, transportation, and production-type jobs that require showing up in person.

Up to 40–50% of employed residents along the wealthier corridor now report working from home at least some of the time. But around Pittsburg, Antioch, and Bay Point, that figure is in the single digits, in some pockets as low as 1–3%.

As of last month, Antioch and Pittsburg/Bay Point have recovered to about 68% and 65% of their pre-pandemic ridership, respectively, outpacing the system average of ~49%.

The proposed closures are only part of what riders would lose. BART also plans to end daily service at 9pm rather than midnight and extend the gaps between trains from 20 minutes to 30.
